Land Partition Law & Co-Ownership Disputes in Sri Lanka

Statutory procedure, preliminary surveys, interlocutory decrees, and final partition decrees under Partition Law No. 21 of 1977.

1. The Problem of Undivided Co-Ownership in Sri Lanka

Undivided co-ownership (commonly referred to as podu idama or undivided shares) is one of the most widespread sources of property paralysis and familial dispute in Sri Lanka. Under Roman-Dutch Law and customary Sri Lankan land tenure, when an owner passes away intestate, all legal heirs inherit an ideal undivided fractional interest in the entire parcel of land, rather than demarcated physical blocks.

Over successive generations, land parcels become fractionated into minuscule fractions (e.g., 7/128, 15/256), leading to severe deadlocks. No single co-owner can build a permanent home, mortgage their share to a commercial bank, or sell to a third-party developer without the unanimous consent of all other co-owners. When amicable private partition through a Deed of Partition is impossible, the statutory remedy is a Partition Action under the Partition Law No. 21 of 1977.

2. Jurisdiction and Statutory Filing Requirements

A partition action must be instituted in the District Court having territorial jurisdiction over the area where the subject land (the corpus) is located (for example, the District Court of Matara for lands in Matara, Weligama, Mirissa, and surrounding areas).

The action is initiated by filing a detailed Plaint, accompanied by:

  • Unbroken Title Pedigree: The plaint must trace the unbroken title devolution starting from the common original owner down to every living co-owner, naming all known claimants as co-defendants.
  • Schedule of the Land: Accurate boundary descriptions, prior registered survey plan references, and the estimated extent of the corpus.
  • Registration of Lis Pendens: Within statutory timeframes, the action must be registered as a lis pendens (pending suit) in the Day Book and folios at the relevant Land Registry to bind any subsequent purchasers or encumbrancers.

Critical Procedural Safeguard: Under Section 12 of the Partition Law, failure to duly register the lis pendens or omit necessary parties from the pedigree can cause the entire action to be dismissed or delayed for years. Experienced senior trial advocacy is essential at the drafting stage.

3. The Preliminary Court Commission Survey

Upon acceptance of the plaint and registration of the lis pendens, the District Court issues a formal Commission to a court-appointed licensed surveyor. The surveyor attends the land after serving advance public notice on all parties and adjoining land owners.

The surveyor's statutory duties include:

  • Surveying the true physical boundaries of the disputed corpus against prior deeds and plans.
  • Recording all existing permanent buildings, agricultural plantations (coconut, tea, cinnamon, rubber, fruit trees), and wells.
  • Noting the distinct possession claimed by each party on the ground.

The resulting Preliminary Survey Plan and comprehensive surveyor's report are formally filed in court and serve as the physical baseline for the entire trial.

4. Trial, Contested Claims & The Interlocutory Decree

Defendants served with summons file statements of claim either agreeing to the pedigree or asserting rival claims (such as prescriptive title under Section 3 of the Prescription Ordinance, or disputed deed devolutions). The District Court examines oral evidence, calls witness testimonies, inspects certified Land Registry folios, and evaluates deed duplicates.

Upon concluding the trial, the District Judge enters an Interlocutory Decree. This judgment judicially defines:

  • The exact undivided fractional share of every declared co-owner.
  • The ownership of specific plantations or houses built by individual co-owners (which grant equitable compensation or preferential allotment rights).
  • Whether the land can be physically divided into separate economic parcels, or whether it must be ordered for Sale by Public Auction.

5. Final Partition Decree vs. Public Auction Sale

Following the interlocutory decree, the case moves to final execution:

  • Physical Partition: If the land is sufficiently large, a court surveyor prepares a Final Partition Scheme dividing the land into demarcated allotments, ensuring road reservations and access. The court confirms the scheme and issues the Final Decree of Partition.
  • Partition Sale: If the land is too narrow, congested with buildings, or below local authority minimum lot-size regulations, the court decrees a sale. A court fiscal or licensed auctioneer sells the land at public auction. Declared co-owners have the right of pre-emption to bid at the auction, and proceeds are deposited in court and distributed to parties according to their decreed shares.

Indefeasible Title: Under Section 48 of the Partition Law, a final decree of partition or a certificate of sale confers an absolute, indefeasible title against the entire world (in rem), completely extinguishing all prior dormant defects, unrecorded family claims, or rival deeds.
